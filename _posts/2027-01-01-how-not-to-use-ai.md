Earlier this year, I read [How Not To Use AI: 50 Contrarian Principles for the Imagination Age](https://www.waterstones.com/book/how-not-to-use-ai/abi-awomosu/9781919191003) by Abi Awomosu. If you saw me during that time, there’s a good chance I’ve fished it out of my bag to recommend it to you. Here are my notes.

## This book will make you think differently

It is a real counterpoint to the current AI narrative. We are currently being told a story about AI that is driven mostly by the big tech companies, but this book reminds us that there is always more than one story.

I found this book incredibly refreshing and I found myself engaging with it in a way I've rarely done since my philosophy degree, including making notes in the margins of where a point struck home or I had a counter-argument. I very much didn’t agree with everything she said, but it was very thought-provoking.

For that reason alone, I have been recommending everyone pick it up, to get a different take. Be warned though: it is a challenging read, in content and writing style, as well as being a daunting size.

Here are some of the points that resonated with me.

## Think for yourself before turning to AI

It's very easy and often tempting to outsource your thinking to AI, and if you're not careful, you could lose those skills.

She addresses this in her first law: "Don't start with silicon—start with soul" (Yes, that is an m-dash. Yes, you might find yourself wondering what support she had with writing this...)

She identifies this as "capitalism following its oldest pattern: find what people need, create dependency, sell it back to them". Here, we are outsourcing thinking itself; and what makes it effective is that it feels like efficiency, like progress.

She has practical suggestions: be clear about what you are asking for before you start, spend time writing on paper before turning to AI, use AI only after you've developed complete thoughts. "Develop your cognitive capacity before adding AI amplification".

^ above secgtion or this secgtion - when you start with AI you optimise for what it can delivery and abandon the intelligence you have that makes you irreplaceable.

## Treating GenAI as purely generative doesn't get best results

She suggests that the term "generative" AI is misleading; it gives the impression that this generates something from nothing. In fact GenAI is trained on billions of pieces of content and reflects patterns back, as well as recombining information to create new patterns not found in the data. She leans into the concept of AI listening to all this content, calling LLMs Large Listening Mediums (rather than Large Language Models): "Every Reddit frustration at 3am. Every Discord workaround revealing unmet needs... Every Amazon review describing exact pain points".

Her point is that if we treat it as if it is intelligently generating genuinely new content, we get generic output, but when we lean into the way it has been trained, we can achieve better outcomes. For example, instead of saying "Give me 10 good marketing strategies" instead we can ask "What patterns do you see in why customers need but do not buy my product?" or "What patterns do you recognise in how our customers express their needs?" Instead of saying "How do we fix our broken process", ask "what's trying to emerge that our current process is blocking?"

This definitely changed my behaviour with GenAI. I now use more questions like "What patterns am I missing?", "How do [$type of audience] typically respond to this?", and this generates better answers to my questions.

## Generic questions get generic answers

Later in the book she suggests doing "prompt poetry". For example: "Show me how desire moves through digital spaces like wind through leaves" and later, showing it other worlds: "Where in nature does coordination happen without management?" She advocates identifying domains where your constraints don't exist: "what pattern makes this constraint irrelevant?", and using the  "pattern trinity": one from nature, one from art and one from an unexpected source (e.g. children's play).

She argues that these pattern-based questions strengthen your own pattern recognition capability.

"The quality of that reflection depends entirely on the consciousness you bring to it. Approach with surface thinking, get surface patterns. Approach with developed consciousness, activate deep pattern recognition across humanity's crystallised wisdom".

## Benefits for neurodiversity

Another thing she points out is that the nature of these tools mean they can work really well for different communication styles. For example, she suggests someone with ADHD may feel frustrated that AI is not following their tangential thinking and lean into the advice that they should 'learn to prompt properly' - i.e. force your brain into linear order, leading to rewriting and deleting prompts several times, ending up with a generic input, whereas the missed opportunity is to lean into stream of consciousness prompting, include all the tangents. "By editing out your cognitive signature, you literally removed the competitive advantage––the unexpected connections that drive real innovation".

She has equivalent suggestions for autism and dyslexia; I found these less sensibe, but 
She calls your unmasked self your cognitive signature and suggests this is what gives you competitive advantage; .

This also ties in with a recommendation she makes earlier in the book to try speaking to AI rather than typing. She says research suggests that speaking activates broader areas of the brain; though like all research she mentions in the book no specific research is cited, and it's not clear that any research into this does genuinely imply that speaking leads to a different quality of thought; however, whatever the science behind it/benefits for different communication styles, I have definitely found that saying everything in an unstructured way can often be a very useful way to progress your thinking using AI.

## Extraction thinking

One theme that comes up throughout the book is AI as extraction thinking. When you approach it as an extraction machine you get spiritually dead answers. She also says "The system extracts twice: first from unpaid creators whose work trains the models, then from underpaid workers who make the output acceptable."

(maybe, this resenated with me as per I can refer to the playing to win)

Her response is to not outsourse our thinking "Every timke you choose to think before you prompt you're committing an act of resistance... they want your thikning externalised, captured, optimised and monetised. They want you dependent on tools they control, convinced you can't function wihtout them". I've definitely seen this, people sometimes feeling that before they answer a question they want to run it past Claude, and this could be a moment to stop and recall what makes you special.

However, the book is not internally consistent on this. On the one hand she points out extraction thinking and that they want our brain, but then later she talks about how to build your council using customGPTs and says, approvingly, "after a month, working without your council will feel like thinking with half your brain". One the one hand - is that actually what we really want?! On the other hand, this internal inconsisentency and feeling like I might want to argue against some o f her theses is what kept this book interesting for me, and helped me use my brain while reading it.

## She proposes a response

It ends with a proposal to grow an alternative garden. "Grow a garden so compelling that people naturally migrate." I love this idea of how to fight back, and in a discussion about this book it led to a really interesting debate about whether we are moving back to the '90s when everyone had a server in their office and we lose the value of centralised information, or whether that would actually be better.

Her idea is, let AI accelereate your thinking and then take that learning into the world to build something real.

## There's so much more

There is so much more in this book that I haven't touched on; for example she talks about the computational inefficiency of the frontier labs' models as being an architectural choice, she talks about the value of questions, "The creative act isn’t in judging outputs but in composing questions” and that The quality of your question determines the quality of your answers, quoting Rumi "Sell your cleverness and buy bewilderment", hallucinations as being a reflection of how we are and an opportunity, and I really appreciated that her examples were very frequently women or people of colour; for example when she’s talkinb aoubt visionaries who sahped our world the list is Ada Lovelace, Octavia Butlei and Grace Hopper.

## This is a heavy tome and hard to get through

This book is self-published, which I can understand: the pace of change in AI means you wouldn't want to wait for a traditional publishing cycle to get this out there.

However, it could really have benefited from a good editor. It's over 500 pages long and does not need to be; her arguments are strong, thought-provoking and refreshing but could have landed stronger in a third of the length.

I did finish it, though if you are struggling to get through it, I'll share that she lost me around chapters 30-35, but I rejoined afterwards and continued to get value from it.

## I recommend this book for challenging your thinking

A few months after reading this, I am still thinking about it and applying some of the lessons. It definitely changed the way I think about and use AI for the better.

The pattern throughout the book is reminding you of your own intelligence and what that brings. Done well, you are getting the AI to amplify, sharpen, enhance, challenge, develop your own thinking. Not do the thinking for you.

It is a challenging read, both in a good way and in a not so good way. At times it is frustrating, and some of the people I've recommended it to have not been happy with me afterwards (sorry Stephen!) but I would still strongly recommend reading some or all of it; if nothing else, it will help you keep your thinking nimble and help you not lose sight of the importance of continuing to think for yourself.
